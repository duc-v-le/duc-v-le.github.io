About

I am an economics PhD candidate at Georgetown University. My research studies how heterogeneous beliefs and trading frictions shape asset prices, with work on speculative bubbles, short-selling costs, and crash risk. Earlier work modeled trade liberalization and growth.

Research

Working papers

  1. “Option Maturity and Stock-Price Bubbles.” (with Dan Cao). Working paper, 2026.
    Abstract
    This paper asks what makes asset-price bubbles emerge and whether they can be detected before they break, a question bearing on financial stability and on real firm investment. It is the first to predict them from firm-level option-trading volume, using accumulated put–call composition across 3,447 U.S. firms (Fama–French 49 industries). Stocks in the top quintile of short-dated call share carry a 24-month-forward crash probability 25.5 percentage points above the bottom quintile; long-dated activity reverses the spread. Ongoing work develops a heterogeneous-belief model with demand-pressure dealers to rationalize these patterns.
  2. “Shorting-Fee Dynamics under Heterogeneous Beliefs.” (with Dan Cao). Working paper, 2026. PDF
    Abstract
    How do short-selling frictions interact with heterogeneous beliefs to shape short exposure and asset-price dynamics? We document an empirical puzzle: borrowing fees rise gradually to a peak and then decline, yet stock prices begin falling well before the fee peak and continue to underperform afterward. To study these joint dynamics, we develop a dynamic general equilibrium model with heterogeneous beliefs where the cost of maintaining a short position increases with its size. Separating physical lending capacity from a convex carrying cost allows short exposure to adjust smoothly. We show that the carrying-cost coefficient primarily disciplines the quantity of short exposure rather than its equilibrium borrowing fee. Because lending capacity remains slack along the equilibrium path, the increasing marginal carrying cost provides the restoring force that generates mean reversion in short exposure. The model captures the qualitative features of this empirical trajectory, establishing a conceptual framework for the co-evolution of asset prices and borrowing costs.

Publications

  1. “Globalization, economic growth, and innovation: A two-country two-period model.” Cuong Le Van, Duc V. Le, and Thanh Tam Nguyen-Huu.
    International Economics 187 (2026), 100725. DOI · arXiv
    Abstract
    This paper examines how a developing country can benefit from trade liberalization. We develop a two-period model, comprising an autarky phase and a globalization phase, and a two-country framework, featuring a developing country and a developed country (representing the rest of the world). Our findings indicate that globalization may disadvantage a developing country when its total factor productivity (TFP) is significantly lower than that of the developed country. However, we demonstrate that the developing country can still achieve gains from trade openness by allocating part of its capital to innovation during the autarky period, thereby enhancing its TFP.
